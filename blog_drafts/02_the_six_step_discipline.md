# The 6-Step Discipline: From a Fleeting Flash of Insight to Daily Practice

### Why clarity evaporates after twenty minutes, and how to build a lasting bridge between intuition and action

A flash of insight is one of the most convincing feelings in the world. 

You draw a card, something clicks, and for twenty minutes you have complete clarity on a dilemma you carried for months. Your chest loosens. You take a full breath. You tell yourself that everything has changed.

Then your phone buzzes. Someone needs an invoice approved, or a colleague pings you with an urgent question. You step away from your desk. By noon, the clarity has evaporated. Not dimmed. Completely gone.

That rapid fade is the central challenge of self-reflection. Insight feels permanent when it strikes, but the human nervous system has short-term memory for abstract realizations. If you don't anchor the breakthrough to a physical routine within hours, your subconscious resets to its default defenses.

The **Leela Self-Reflections Practice** is designed to close that gap. It takes the three cards of the StOR framework (State, Obstacle, Resource) and channels them through a six-step process, moving from initial fog to a daily habit you can actually sustain.

---

## Step 1: Self-Clarification (The Query)

Everything starts with honest silence. 

Before touching the cards, you have to peel away the noise and name the tension keeping you awake. If you skip this step, you end up looping in generalities. 

A useful query refuses to look outward. Asking *"Will my business succeed?"* or *"Why is my partner distant?"* hands all your power to circumstances you cannot control. Shift the question until you have skin in the game. Instead of asking what the other person is thinking, ask: *"What unresolved part of myself is being triggered right now?"* 

Name your emotional stake. Once you feel that slight discomfort in your gut, you are ready.

---

## Step 2: Synchronicity (The Draw)

With your question in mind, draw three cards at random from the 72 states of consciousness.

Randomness provides what psychologists call psychological distancing. When you are tangled in an emotional problem, your ego is in defensive mode. Looking at an abstract metaphor - whether it is *Krodha* (Anger) or *Dharma* (Inner Truth) - lets you inspect your situation from a safe vantage point without immediately flinching.

* **State:** Marks your actual starting line.
* **Obstacle:** Identifies your blind spot. This step demands the most courage. The natural urge is to glance at the obstacle and hurry toward the solution, because looking at our own pettiness or fear stings. Linger here anyway.
* **Resource:** Uncovers the one internal tool you genuinely trust.

---

## Step 3: Personal Interpretation (Internal Dialogue)

Having three cards on the table means nothing until your own mind makes the connection.

Each card carries classic reflective questions suited to its position. But you cannot treat them like a textbook definition. You have to interpret the symbol directly through the lens of your actual struggle.

Suppose your Obstacle card is *Greed*. The knee-jerk reaction is to dismiss it: *"I'm not greedy. I don't even care that much about money."* 

Look closer. Greed isn't just about cash. In shadow work, greed usually looks like a greed for control, a greed for certainty, or a desperate greed for everyone in the room to approve of you. What are you hoarding? 

This internal dialogue bridges ancient archetypes and the messy reality of your life. It isn't about getting the "right" scholarly definition. It is about watching how your mind naturally links the symbol to your current pain point.

---

## Step 4: Fixation (The Journal)

Write it down. Put actual words onto paper or type them into a dedicated log.

Thoughts are slippery. If an insight stays inside your head, your conscious mind will gradually edit it over the next three days until it fits your comfortable old narrative. Writing forces abstract, emotional waves into linear syntax. The moment you translate a feeling into a complete sentence, you strip away its power to haunt you in the dark.

Keep it raw. Don't write an essay for an audience. Write down the sentence you were hoping you wouldn't have to admit.

---

## Step 5: Self-Analysis (The Root Conclusion)

Read back what you just wrote, and distill it down to a single core takeaway.

Researcher Donald Schön studied how master professionals work - people like architects, clinicians, and jazz musicians who rely heavily on intuition. He discovered that their mastery did not come from raw talent. It came from a disciplined habit he named *Reflective Practice*: pausing in the middle of action to ask why something worked or why it failed. 

Raw experience alone teaches us very little. We repeat the same dysfunctional patterns for decades while calling it "life experience." Growth only happens when you deliberately reflect on what that experience is trying to show you.

In this step, you step into Schön's observer seat. Look at your journal notes with calm detachment. Strip away the emotional drama until one clear conclusion remains.

---

## Step 6: Personal Discipline (The Micro-Action)

A breakthrough without an action is just spiritual entertainment.

The grandest realization on earth dissolves if it doesn't change your schedule. The fatal mistake people make here is setting huge, vague resolutions: *"I am going to be more patient"* or *"I will stop being anxious."* Those aren't actions; they are wishes. When Tuesday morning arrives, wishes collapse under stress.

Take your Root Conclusion and the Resource card, and compress them into a single, concrete micro-action:

* If your insight calls for **Inner Peace**, don't just pledge to stay calm. Commit to a specific physical habit: *"Tonight, before opening any social media app in bed, I will spend two minutes visualizing how I want tomorrow morning to feel."*
* If the insight calls for **Courage**, give it a boundary: *"Before checking my morning email, I will spend five minutes working on the presentation I've been dreading."*

Tie the micro-action to an existing physical anchor - your morning coffee, your car keys, the moment your hand touches the office door handle. 

That small, stubborn habit is what carries your insight through the noise of the day. It turns an abstract revelation into lived muscle memory.
